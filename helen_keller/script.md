# The Life of Helen Keller - English script (about 30 minutes)
# Lines starting with '#' are not spoken. Blank lines separate paragraphs.

# Intro
Tuscumbia, Alabama, April 5th, 1887. Behind an old farmhouse, a young woman pumped cold water over the hand of a little girl. Into the girl's other hand, she spelled a word with her fingers. W, A, T, E, R. Then she spelled it again, faster.

The girl was six years old. She could not see. She could not hear. For almost five years, she had lived in a world without light, without sound, and without words. She had kicked, screamed, and broken things, because she had no other way to say what she wanted.

At that pump, she suddenly understood that the movements in her hand meant the cool thing pouring over her skin. Everything had a name. Later she wrote, "That living word awakened my soul, gave it light, hope, joy, set it free."

That girl would learn to read in five languages, graduate from one of the best colleges in America, write more than a dozen books, and travel to almost forty countries. She would meet every American president from Grover Cleveland to Lyndon Johnson. She would also fight for workers, for women, and for millions of blind people around the world. Her name was Helen Keller. Today, we follow her life from beginning to end.

# Chapter 1 Ivy Green
Helen Adams Keller was born on June 27th, 1880, in Tuscumbia, a small town in northern Alabama. Her family lived on a farm called Ivy Green. The main house was a simple white home, surrounded by English ivy, roses, and honeysuckle.

Her father, Arthur Keller, had been a captain in the Confederate army during the Civil War. After the war, he ran a cotton farm and edited the local newspaper. Her mother, Kate, was almost twenty years younger than her husband. She was educated, proud, and determined.

Helen was their first child together, and by every account, a bright and lively baby. She walked on the day she turned one. Before she was a year and a half old, she had already begun to say a few words. One of them was "water."

# Chapter 2 The Silent Dark
In February 1882, when Helen was nineteen months old, she fell sick with a high fever. Doctors at the time called it a "congestion of the stomach and brain." Today, many believe it was scarlet fever or meningitis. For days, her parents thought she would die.

Then, one morning, the fever was gone. Her mother was overjoyed. But a few days later, she noticed something strange. When the dinner bell rang, Helen did not turn her head. When her mother passed a hand in front of her eyes, Helen did not blink. The illness had left her completely blind and completely deaf.

As she grew, Helen made up her own signs to talk with her family. A push meant "go." A pull meant "come." Shivering meant "cold." To ask for her father, she pretended to put on a pair of glasses. By the age of five, she had about sixty signs. But she knew there was something more. She would stand between people who were talking and touch their moving lips. She moved her own lips, and nothing happened. Then she would fly into a rage.

Her tantrums grew worse every year. She grabbed food from other people's plates at the dinner table. She once locked her mother in the pantry and sat on the porch, feeling the door shake as her mother pounded on it. When her baby sister Mildred was born, Helen tipped over the cradle. Some relatives quietly said that the child should be sent to an asylum.

# Chapter 3 A Letter to Boston
Kate Keller refused to give up. One day, she read a book by the English writer Charles Dickens, called American Notes. In it, Dickens described a girl named Laura Bridgman, who was both blind and deaf. Fifty years earlier, at a school in Boston, Laura had learned to read and write.

In 1886, the Kellers took Helen to a famous eye doctor in Baltimore. He could do nothing for her eyes. But he told them to see a man in Washington who worked with deaf children. That man was Alexander Graham Bell, the inventor of the telephone.

Bell was kind to Helen. He let her feel his pocket watch ring. Years later, she said that he had opened the door through which she would pass from darkness into light. Bell told her father to write to the Perkins Institution for the Blind in Boston, the same school where Laura Bridgman had been taught.

The director of Perkins, Michael Anagnos, read the letter. He had a former student in mind. She was twenty years old, she had just graduated at the top of her class, and she had no job. Her name was Anne Sullivan.

# Chapter 4 Annie
Anne Sullivan's own life had been a story of hardship. She was born in 1866 in Massachusetts, to poor Irish immigrants. When she was five, an eye disease called trachoma began to destroy her sight. When she was eight, her mother died. Her father, a heavy drinker, soon abandoned the family.

At ten, Annie and her little brother Jimmie were sent to the state poorhouse in Tewksbury. It was a crowded, filthy place, full of the sick, the old, and the insane. Jimmie had a bad hip, and only a few months later, he died there. Annie was left alone.

She stayed in the poorhouse for four years. Then, in 1880, a group of state officials came to inspect it. Annie threw herself in front of them and begged to be sent to school. A few months later, at fourteen, she arrived at Perkins. She could not read or write, and she did not even know how to spell her own name.

At Perkins, surgeons operated on her eyes, and her sight improved enough for her to read. She was quick, stubborn, and often in trouble. But in June 1886, she gave the graduation speech for her class. Months later, she packed her bag and took the train south to Alabama.

# Chapter 5 The Battle
Anne Sullivan arrived at Ivy Green on March 3rd, 1887. Helen later called it her soul's birthday. Helen was standing on the porch. She felt footsteps coming toward her, and reached out her hand, thinking it was her mother.

The next morning, Annie gave Helen a doll, a gift from the blind girls at Perkins. Then she spelled the word "doll" into her hand. Helen copied the movements of the fingers. She was proud of herself, but she had no idea that she was spelling a word. To her, it was only a game.

The real battle came at the breakfast table. Helen walked around, grabbing food from every plate. When she reached for Annie's plate, Annie refused to let her. Helen threw herself on the floor, kicking and screaming. Annie asked the family to leave the room, and locked the door. For the next hour, they fought. In the end, Helen sat in her chair, ate with a spoon, and folded her napkin.

Annie realized that she could not teach Helen as long as her parents gave in to her every wish. She asked for Helen to live with her alone. The family let them move into a small cottage near the main house. For two weeks, Annie spelled words into Helen's hand all day long. Helen calmed down. She learned to trust her teacher. But she still did not understand what words were.

# Chapter 6 Water
On the morning of April 5th, 1887, Annie and Helen argued over two words, "mug" and "water." Helen kept confusing them. In anger, she threw her new doll on the floor and smashed it.

Annie took her outside, to the water pump near the well house. Someone was pumping water, and Annie held Helen's hand under the cold stream. Into her other hand, she spelled the word "water," first slowly, then quickly.

Helen stood very still. Suddenly, she understood. The word in her hand was the name of the wonderful cool thing flowing over her skin. Everything had a name. She dropped to the ground, touched it, and asked for its name. She touched the pump, and the fence. Then she turned and touched Annie, and Annie spelled "teacher." For the rest of her life, Helen called her by that name.

By that evening, Helen had learned thirty new words. That night, she went back to the pieces of the broken doll and tried, without success, to put them together. For the first time in her life, she felt sorry for what she had done. Within a few months, she knew hundreds of words, and she was learning to read raised letters and Braille.

# Chapter 7 The Wonder Child
Michael Anagnos was thrilled. In his reports, he wrote about the little girl from Alabama as a miracle. Newspapers across America and Europe picked up the story. By the age of eight, Helen was famous.

In 1888, Helen and Annie traveled to Boston, and Helen began studying at Perkins. For the first time, she could talk with children who knew the finger alphabet. She read every book she could find in Braille. She was learning French, Latin, and Greek.

At Perkins, Helen also met Laura Bridgman, the woman whose story had first given her mother hope. Laura was now almost sixty, and she still lived at the school. The two deafblind women spelled into each other's hands. But Laura was stiff and strict, and she scolded the excited little girl for being too rough. It was a strange meeting between the past and the future.

But Helen wanted more than spelling with her hands. She wanted to speak. In 1890, at the age of nine, she began taking lessons from Sarah Fuller, the head of a school for the deaf in Boston. Helen put her hand on her teacher's face, feeling how her tongue and lips moved, and how her throat vibrated.

After only eleven lessons, she said her first full sentence: "I am not dumb now." For the rest of her life, she would practice speaking. Her voice always sounded unusual, and strangers often found it hard to follow her. But she never stopped trying.

# Chapter 8 The Frost King
In the fall of 1891, eleven-year-old Helen wrote a short story called "The Frost King" and sent it to Anagnos as a birthday present. He loved it, and had it printed in a magazine.

Soon, someone noticed that it was very similar to a story called "The Frost Fairies," published years before Helen was born. It seems that someone had once read that story to Helen, and that it had stayed in her memory. She was certain she had made it up herself.

Perkins held a kind of trial. A group of teachers questioned the child for two hours, without Annie in the room. In the end, the vote was split evenly, and Anagnos cast the deciding vote in Helen's favor. But their friendship was broken. Helen cried herself to sleep that night. For years afterward, she was afraid to write anything, in case the words were not her own.

# Chapter 9 Mark Twain
In 1894, Helen and Annie moved to New York, where Helen studied at a school for the deaf. There, at the age of fourteen, she met one of the most famous writers in the world, Mark Twain. He treated her not as a curiosity, but as a friend. He told her stories, and she read his lips with her fingers.

Helen had a dream. She wanted to go to college, just like other young women. But her father had died in 1896, and the family had little money. Twain asked a rich friend, the oil businessman Henry Rogers, to help. Rogers and his wife agreed to pay for her education.

Twain once compared Annie Sullivan to a miracle worker. He is said to have called Helen and Napoleon the two most interesting characters of the nineteenth century. Their friendship lasted until his death, sixteen years after they met.

# Chapter 10 Radcliffe
To get into college, Helen first went to a preparatory school in Cambridge, Massachusetts. In 1899, she passed the entrance exams for Radcliffe College, the women's college of Harvard University. The next year, she began her classes there.

College was hard. Few of her books were printed in Braille. In every lecture, Annie sat beside her and spelled everything into her hand, as fast as the professor could speak. At night, Annie read the books to her, page after page, ruining her own weak eyes. Helen typed her essays on a typewriter.

While she was still a student, she wrote the story of her own life. It was published in 1903 as The Story of My Life. It became a bestseller, and is still read today, in more than fifty languages.

In June 1904, Helen Keller graduated from Radcliffe with honors. She was twenty-four years old. She became the first deafblind person ever to earn a college degree. Annie sat beside her at the ceremony, but the diploma bore only Helen's name.

# Chapter 11 The World Through Her Fingers
People often asked Helen how she knew the world around her. The answer was through touch, smell, and taste. She recognized her friends by the shape of their hands, and could tell their mood by the way they shook hers. She knew when a door slammed somewhere in the house, because she felt the floor tremble under her feet.

To follow what people said, she often placed her thumb on the speaker's lips and her fingers along the jaw and throat. In this way, she could understand a conversation, slowly, without anyone spelling into her hand.

Her sense of smell was remarkable. She said she could often tell what work a person did from the scent of their clothes. A carpenter smelled of wood, a painter of paint, and an iron worker of metal. She could smell a storm coming, hours before it arrived. In 1908, she described all of this in a book called The World I Live In.

She loved being outdoors. She swam, rowed a boat, rode a bicycle built for two, and played chess and checkers on boards made especially for her. As a child, she had written that the best and most beautiful things in the world cannot be seen or even touched. They must be felt with the heart.

# Chapter 12 The Radical
After college, Helen and Annie bought a farmhouse in Wrentham, Massachusetts. In 1905, Annie married John Macy, a young Harvard teacher who had helped Helen edit her book. The three of them lived together.

Helen began to read about the causes of blindness. She learned that many poor people lost their sight because of dangerous factories, bad housing, and disease. She came to believe that poverty itself was the enemy. In 1909, she joined the Socialist Party.

She spoke and wrote for workers' rights, for votes for women, and against child labor. She opposed America entering the First World War. She would later help found the American Civil Liberties Union. Many Americans were shocked. The newspapers that had once called her a saint now said that her opinions came from the limits of her disabilities.

One editor wrote that her mistakes came from the "manifest limitations of her development." Helen answered sharply. When she had met him years before, she wrote, he had praised her intelligence. Now that she held opinions he disliked, he suddenly remembered that she was blind and deaf. It was his newspaper, she said, that was socially blind and deaf. The FBI would later keep a file on her political activities.

# Chapter 13 Love and Money
By 1914, Annie's marriage had fallen apart, and John Macy moved out. Annie's health was failing. A young Scottish woman named Polly Thomson came to work as Helen's secretary. She would stay with Helen for almost fifty years.

In 1916, while Annie was ill, a twenty-nine-year-old man named Peter Fagan was hired as Helen's secretary. He was kind and clever, and they fell in love. They secretly applied for a marriage license. But a newspaper found out and printed the story. Helen's mother was furious. Her family chased Peter away, and the plan to marry collapsed. Helen never married. Later she wrote that this brief love had been "a little island of joy, surrounded by dark waters."

Money was a constant worry. In 1919, Helen starred in a Hollywood film about her own life, called Deliverance. It failed at the box office. So, from 1920, Helen and Annie went on the vaudeville stage. Between jugglers and comedians, Annie told the story of their life, and Helen answered questions from the audience. Asked once whether she closed her eyes when she slept, she replied that she never stayed awake to see. For four years, they toured the country, and earned more than ever before.

# Chapter 14 A Voice for the Blind
In 1915, during the First World War, Helen helped found an organization to help soldiers who had been blinded in battle. It still exists today, under the name Helen Keller International, and works to prevent blindness and hunger in many countries.

In 1924, Helen began working for the new American Foundation for the Blind. She would work for it for more than forty years. She gave speeches across America, raised millions of dollars, and spoke to Congress. She helped persuade the government to pay for books for blind readers, and to support blind people who needed help.

That same year, she wrote a famous letter about music. She had placed her hand on a radio while an orchestra played Beethoven's Ninth Symphony. Through the vibrations, she said, she could feel the different instruments, and the voices of the choir rising and falling like a flame.

In 1933, the Nazis in Germany burned books they hated. One of them was a collection of Helen's essays about socialism. She wrote an open letter to the students of Germany. "You can burn my books and the books of the best minds in Europe," she wrote, "but the ideas in them have seeped through a million channels, and will continue to quicken other minds."

# Chapter 15 Teacher
By the early 1930s, Annie Sullivan was nearly blind herself. She was often in pain, and tired. Helen and Polly cared for her, as Annie had once cared for Helen.

Anne Sullivan died on October 20th, 1936, in New York, with Helen holding her hand. She was seventy years old. For almost fifty years, the two women had hardly ever been apart. Her ashes were placed at the Washington National Cathedral. She was the first woman honored there for her own achievements.

Helen was fifty-six. She said that she felt as if she had lost her eyes and ears a second time. For the rest of her life, she would never stop speaking about the woman she simply called "Teacher."

# Chapter 16 Across the Ocean
Only a few months after Annie's death, Helen and Polly sailed for Japan. Annie had once promised to go with her. Helen kept the promise alone. For three months, she traveled across the country, gave nearly a hundred speeches, and was welcomed by huge crowds.

In July 1937, she crossed the sea to Korea, which was then under Japanese rule. For one week, she traveled from Busan to Daegu, Seoul, and Pyongyang. In Seoul, she spoke in a large public hall in the center of the city, and the tickets sold out. Korean newspapers covered her every step. Historians now note that the Japanese colonial authorities also tried to use her visit for their own purposes.

In Japan, Helen heard the story of Hachiko, the loyal Akita dog who had waited at Shibuya station for his dead master for almost ten years. She asked for an Akita of her own. A police officer gave her a puppy named Kamikaze-go. He became the first Akita ever brought to America. Sadly, he died only months later. The Japanese government then sent her his brother, Kenzan-go.

# Chapter 17 Fire and War
In 1939, Helen and Polly moved into a new home in Easton, Connecticut. She named it Arcan Ridge. Every morning, she walked alone along a long rail that had been built for her in the garden.

During the Second World War, Helen visited wounded American soldiers in military hospitals across the country. Many had lost their sight or their hearing in battle. She sat beside them, touched their faces, and told them that their lives were not over.

In 1946, while Helen was traveling in Europe, Arcan Ridge burned to the ground. Almost everything she owned was lost, including her library of Braille books and the manuscript of a book she had been writing about Annie for years. She started again from the beginning. Friends helped rebuild the house.

After the war, she began traveling the world for blind people in poorer countries. Over eleven years, she visited thirty-five countries on five continents. In 1948, she went back to Japan, and walked through the ruins of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. She was nearly seventy years old.

# Chapter 18 The Miracle Worker
In 1955, Helen finally published her book about Annie. It was called simply Teacher. The same year, a documentary about her life, Helen Keller in Her Story, won an Academy Award.

Two years later, a writer named William Gibson turned the story of Helen and Annie into a television play, The Miracle Worker. In 1959, it opened on Broadway, with Anne Bancroft as Annie and a young actress named Patty Duke as Helen. Every night, the audience watched the fight at the breakfast table, and the moment at the water pump.

In 1962, it became a film. Both actresses won Academy Awards. A new generation of people around the world came to know the story of the little girl and her teacher.

# Chapter 19 The Last Years
In 1960, Polly Thomson died. Helen was now eighty, and had lost the last of the two women who had been her eyes, her ears, and her voice for most of her life. A nurse named Winnie Corbally took care of her.

In 1961, Helen suffered a series of strokes. She retired from public life, and spent her last years quietly at Arcan Ridge, reading, and receiving visits from friends. In 1964, President Lyndon Johnson awarded her the Presidential Medal of Freedom, the highest honor an American civilian can receive.

Helen Keller died in her sleep on June 1st, 1968, at Arcan Ridge. She was eighty-seven years old, just a few weeks short of her eighty-eighth birthday. Her ashes were placed at the Washington National Cathedral, beside those of Anne Sullivan and Polly Thomson.

Her story did not end with her death. In 2003, the state of Alabama put her image on its quarter coin, with her name written in Braille. It was the first American coin in general circulation to carry Braille. In 2009, a bronze statue of Helen was placed in the United States Capitol in Washington. It shows her not as an old woman, but as a seven-year-old girl, standing at the water pump.

She had been called a saint, a miracle, and a radical. For much of her life, people loved the little girl at the water pump, but did not want to hear what the grown woman had to say. She spoke anyway, about poverty, about war, and about the rights of women and workers. She showed the world what people with disabilities could do, if they were only given the chance.

The little girl who once had no words left behind millions of them. As she once wrote, "Life is either a daring adventure, or nothing."
